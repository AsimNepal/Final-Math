01 / THE IDEA

Why I wanted to study this

I grew up in Nepal and later moved to the United States for college. Seeing different roads and traffic made me wonder how road networks affect the choices people make.

One simple question

When a city builds another road, most people expect traffic to improve. But a road network is more complicated than a single road. A new connection can change the routes that thousands of people choose.

I wanted to understand that change with equations instead of guessing.

Why I used different math subjects

This project brings together ideas I have studied in calculus, discrete mathematics, and linear algebra. Graphs describe the roads. Matrices count drivers. Derivatives and integrals explain travel time. Optimization helps find the best result for the whole network.

It is an independent learning project based on a known mathematical result called Braess's paradox.

BRAESS'S PARADOX / THE CENTRAL IDEA

What is Braess's Paradox?

Braess's Paradox is a surprising mathematical result: adding a new road can sometimes make everyone's journey longer. German mathematician Dietrich Braess described it in 1968.

Why does it happen?

Each driver wants the fastest available route. When a new road opens, it may look like a shortcut, so drivers change their routes. But those choices also affect traffic on other roads.

In the end, everyone may take longer than before, even though no single driver can save time by switching routes alone. This is called a traffic equilibrium.

What makes it a paradox?

We usually expect more roads to improve traffic. Braess's Paradox shows why that is not always true: a choice that looks best for one driver may make the result worse for everyone.

This connects traffic networks to graph theory, game theory, and optimization.

HOW IT CONNECTS TO MY PROJECT

When does the paradox happen?

In my starting model, 4,000 drivers take 65 minutes each before a new road opens. After it opens, the equilibrium travel time rises to 80 minutes.

I then changed how quickly roads slow down as traffic increases. In my model, adding the road makes trips longer when the road parameter k is greater than 4000/90 and less than 6000/45, approximately 44.44 to 133.33.

03 / HOW THE MATHEMATICS WORKS

One traffic problem, several kinds of mathematics

I wanted to use the ideas from my math courses together, rather than treat each subject as a separate topic.

DISCRETE MATHEMATICS

Roads become a graph

The places A, B, C, and D are vertices. Roads are directed edges because drivers can travel in a certain direction. Before the new road, there are two paths from A to D. Adding the road creates a third path.

G = (V, E)

This gives a clear way to describe the network before calculating anything.

LINEAR ALGEBRA

A matrix counts drivers

Each column of the route matrix represents one possible trip. Each row represents one road. Multiplying the matrix by the route flows tells us how many drivers use every road.

f = Rp

For example, drivers on the upper road include people taking the upper route and the new middle route.

CALCULUS I AND II

Derivatives and integrals

A derivative tells us how quickly travel time rises when more drivers enter a road. An integral helps build a function whose minimum describes the traffic equilibrium for this type of network.

t'(x) = 1/k

The integral used for equilibrium is different from the total time spent by all drivers.

CALCULUS III

Finding the best split

We can choose how many drivers take each route and calculate the total time spent in traffic. Partial derivatives help find the split that makes the total as small as possible.

∇C(a,b) = 0

The best split for everyone is not always the one drivers reach by choosing routes on their own.

ORIGINAL MODEL

The equilibrium and the best result are different

With the new road, the equilibrium is 80 minutes per driver. But if we could plan how the drivers are split between the three routes, the lowest possible average would be 64.6875 minutes.

At that best split, 1,750 drivers take each outer route and 500 take the middle route. This is a useful example of how a decision that seems best for one person can make things worse for the whole group.

STEP 1

Before adding the road

Drivers split evenly between two equal routes. Each has 2,000 drivers. The travel time is 20 minutes on a crowded road plus 45 minutes on a fixed road.

20 + 45 = 65 min
STEP 2

After adding the road

In the original model, all 4,000 drivers choose the new middle route. That trip takes 40 minutes on each crowded road. Switching alone would take 85 minutes, so no driver wants to change.

40 + 40 = 80 min
STEP 3

Measure the difference

Adding the road raises the trip from 65 minutes to 80 minutes. That is 15 extra minutes, or about a 23 percent increase.

(80 − 65) / 65 ≈ 23.08%

THE MATHEMATICAL CONDITION

When does the new road actually hurt?

Instead of checking only two examples, I let the road parameter k change. By solving the traffic equilibrium in three cases and comparing the times before and after, I found the exact range where the new road makes traffic worse.

44.44 < k < 133.33

This is an approximate form. The exact bounds are 4000/90 and 6000/45. Below the first bound, the new road makes no difference. Above the second bound, it helps.

05 / RESEARCH EXTENSION

Beyond the four-road example

I expanded the project to study how traffic demand and network structure affect the paradox. The Python experiments compare the routes drivers choose themselves with the routes that minimize total travel time.

EXACT MATHEMATICAL RESULT

Changing the number of drivers

For the classic network, I derived that the new road makes equilibrium travel slower when demand is strictly between 3,000 and 9,000 drivers. Outside that range, adding the road does not make equilibrium travel worse in this model.

This is a result for the established Braess network, not a claim of a new theorem.

EQUILIBRIUM VS OPTIMUM

Measuring network inefficiency

At 4,000 drivers, user equilibrium takes 80 minutes, while a system-optimal allocation averages 64.6875 minutes. Their ratio, called the price of anarchy, is approximately 1.237.

The calculations use a Python optimizer and are checked against the exact four-node solution.

What happens in larger networks?

I tested 192 synthetic network-demand scenarios across two network families. Random layered networks showed no paradox cases in this sample (0/96), while networks deliberately built around a Braess structure showed 48/96. These numbers describe only the models I generated, not real traffic networks.

What this project taught me

A small change can affect a whole system
